How AI Virtual Try-On Technology Actually Works

Less machinery than the marketing suggests, and more interesting for it. Here is the real pipeline behind a hairstyle try-on in 2026 — and the parts that genuinely go wrong.

A correction, August 2026. An earlier version of this article described a five-stage pipeline with 68-point 3D facial landmarking, semantic hair segmentation and an in-house GAN "trained on millions of hairstyle images". We do not train a model, and that is not how our app works. The article has been rewritten to describe what actually happens. We are leaving the correction visible rather than quietly swapping the text.

The short version

A modern hairstyle try-on is one call to a large image model, given two pictures: your selfie, and a photograph of the hairstyle. The model draws a new photograph of you wearing it. Almost everything else is plumbing.

That is a much smaller claim than "proprietary neural style transfer", and it is the truthful one. The interesting engineering is not in the model — it is in what you feed it.

Why the reference photograph is the whole game

Every style in our catalogue is a real photograph, and that photograph is the specification. When you tap a style, the app fetches that exact image and hands it to the model as the frame to copy.

This sounds obvious. It was not how we built it originally. The first version described each style in words — a written blurb saying "medium-length knotless braids, waist length, dark brown" — and asked the model to work from the description. It produced plausible braids that were not the braids in the picture you tapped. People do not pick a style from a paragraph; they pick it because they saw it. If the words and the photograph disagree, the photograph is what the user meant.

So the pipeline now derives its own structured read of the style from the same photograph the user saw, and that read, not the marketing copy, is what shapes the request. When a written description contradicts the image, the image wins.

What actually runs, in order

  1. You give it a face. A selfie from your camera or gallery, uploaded over TLS.
  2. The app fetches the style's reference photograph and extracts a structured description of what is in it — length, parting, thickness, texture, how the style sits on the head.
  3. Both images plus that description go to the image model. Ours is Google's Gemini. We do not train our own model, and very few apps in this category do; the ones that say they do are usually describing a licensed API in dramatic language.
  4. The model generates a new photograph. Not a composite, not an overlay — new pixels, which is why it can follow the lighting and angle of your original shot.
  5. The result comes back, is stored with your account, and appears in the app. If the model returns an empty or unusable frame, the app retries before showing you a failure.
Generated pixels, not a wig sticker. That is the difference you can actually see — and the reason the same request twice will not give you the identical image.

Why results vary — and why that is inherent

Image models are probabilistic. Running the same selfie against the same style twice gives two slightly different photographs. That is not a bug being hidden from you; it is what this class of model is.

What makes the difference between a good result and a bad one, in rough order of impact:

  • Your photo. Front-facing, evenly lit, whole head in frame. A dark, steeply angled or cropped shot is the single most common cause of a poor result.
  • Obstruction. Hands in the hair, hats, heavy filters and sunglasses all degrade it.
  • How unusual the style is. Common silhouettes are more reliable than rare, highly structured ones.
  • Server conditions. Generation happens remotely. Half of our recent looks returned within 24 seconds and nine in ten within 51 seconds, but a slow one is a slow one.

What this technology cannot do

Worth being blunt, because the category oversells this badly:

  • It cannot tell you whether your own hair is long or dense enough for a style. It draws the outcome, not the route to it.
  • It is not a measurement. Do not take a generated length as what your braider will produce.
  • It does not preserve fine identity detail perfectly. Look at a result closely and you will find small departures from your face.
  • It is not real-time. Live AR mirrors run a completely different, much lighter class of model, and they look it.

What happens to your photo

The honest answer, for our app specifically:

  • Your photo is uploaded over an encrypted connection.
  • It is stored with your account, so your past looks are there when you come back. An earlier version of this page said photos were deleted straight after processing. That was wrong, and we would rather correct it than leave you with a false impression of where your face is.
  • Two processors handle it: Google's Gemini, which draws the new hairstyle, and Cloudinary, which hosts the images.
  • We do not sell your photos and do not use them for advertising.
  • Email support@hairstudioapp.com and we will delete your photos and results.

When you evaluate any app in this category, that list is the one to ask for. "Processed securely" is not an answer — it names no processor, no retention period and no way out.

Key takeaways

AI hairstyle try-on is a licensed image model, a carefully chosen reference photograph, and a lot of unglamorous work making sure the picture you tapped is the picture the model is asked to copy. The apps that get it right are the ones whose catalogue is honest, not the ones with the most impressive-sounding architecture diagram.

If an app tells you it built its own GAN and trained it on millions of images, ask which images, and who owns them. We got that answer wrong on this page once. It is worth asking.
